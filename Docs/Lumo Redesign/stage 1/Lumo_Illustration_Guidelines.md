# Lumo Illustration Guidelines

**Document:** LUMO_ILLUSTRATION_GUIDELINES.md  
**Status:** Locked — Stage 1 Design System  
**Product:** Lumo  
**Purpose:** Define the visual language for illustrations, educational diagrams, AI tutor visuals, simulations, environments, and reusable learning assets.

---

# 1. Purpose

Lumo is not a conventional educational website.

Its visual system should make difficult concepts feel:

- Clear
- Intuitive
- Interesting
- Premium
- Memorable
- Visually understandable

Illustrations are therefore not decorative elements.

They are part of Lumo's **teaching system**.

Every visual should answer at least one of these questions:

> What is the student supposed to understand from this?

> What relationship is the visual making easier to understand?

> What would be harder to understand without the visual?

If a visual does not improve comprehension, orientation, engagement, or emotional connection, it should generally not exist.

---

# 2. Core Art Direction

Lumo's visual direction is:

> **Editorial educational visualization + cinematic interaction + intelligent simplicity.**

The visual language should feel like a combination of:

- A premium digital textbook
- A modern scientific visualization platform
- An interactive classroom
- A cinematic educational documentary
- A sophisticated AI product

It should NOT feel like:

- Generic children's educational software
- Stock illustration libraries
- Generic AI-generated imagery
- Corporate SaaS illustrations
- Random 3D objects
- Cartoon-heavy children's games
- Decorative AI slop
- Excessive gradients
- Generic talking-avatar interfaces

---

# 3. The Core Visual Principle

## Teach first. Impress second.

Lumo should absolutely look impressive.

However, visual sophistication must serve learning.

A beautiful animation that communicates nothing is worse than a simple diagram that makes a difficult concept immediately obvious.

The hierarchy is:

1. Understanding
2. Clarity
3. Visual storytelling
4. Engagement
5. Aesthetic sophistication

Never reverse this order.

---

# 4. Visual Hierarchy

Every educational visual should have a clear hierarchy.

A student should immediately understand:

1. What is the main object?
2. What relationship is being demonstrated?
3. What is changing?
4. What should they pay attention to?
5. What conclusion should they draw?

Avoid presenting multiple equally prominent elements simultaneously.

Example:

Bad:

```text
CAR
TREE
PERSON
SUN
ROAD
ARROW
FORMULA
TEXT
ICON
LABEL
GRAPH
```

Everything competes for attention.

Better:

```text
                    FORCE

                      ↓

              ┌─────────────┐
              │    CART     │ ─────→
              └─────────────┘
                         motion

                     F = ma
```

The student knows exactly what to look at.

# 5. Visual Language

Lumo illustrations should generally use:

- Clean silhouettes
- Controlled detail
- Strong spatial hierarchy
- Soft geometry
- Consistent proportions
- Clear edges
- Purposeful labels
- Restrained decoration
- Natural depth
- Carefully controlled lighting

Visuals should feel intentionally designed rather than randomly generated.

# 6. Illustration Styles

Lumo supports several visual styles.

These styles should not be mixed arbitrarily.

## 6.1 Editorial 2D

Best for:

- Biology diagrams
- History
- Geography
- Concept explanations
- Infographics
- Mathematical explanations
- Simplified scientific diagrams

Characteristics:

- Clean linework
- Flat or lightly shaded surfaces
- Limited visual noise
- Clear labels
- Controlled color usage
- Strong composition

---

## 6.2 Dimensional 2.5D

Best for:

- Physics
- Engineering
- Geography
- Architecture
- Chemistry
- Environmental explanations

Characteristics:

- Slight depth
- Layered objects
- Soft shadows
- Perspective
- More realistic spatial relationships
- Still visually simple enough for education

This should often be the default for concepts that benefit from depth without requiring full 3D.

---

## 6.3 Interactive 3D

Best for:

- Human anatomy
- Cells
- Molecules
- Solar systems
- Mechanical systems
- Engineering components
- Geometry
- Spatial mathematics
- Architecture
- Physical simulations

3D should be used when **spatial understanding is genuinely improved by it**.

3D should not exist simply because it looks impressive.

---

## 6.4 Cinematic Environmental Scenes

Best for:

- Real-world examples
- Physics scenarios
- Geography
- Environmental science
- History
- Economics
- Everyday applications

Examples:

```text
Physics
→ shopping cart
→ car
→ football
→ falling object

Biology
→ forest
→ ecosystem
→ pond
→ human body

Geography
→ mountain
→ river
→ city
→ climate environment

Economics
→ market
→ shop
→ factory
→ household
```

These scenes should support the explanation rather than becoming elaborate background artwork.

# 7. Subject-Specific Visual Strategy

Lumo must adapt its visual language to the subject.

There is no single universal illustration style for every topic.

---

# 8. Physics

Physics visuals should prioritize:

- Motion
- Forces
- Vectors
- Relationships
- Measurements
- Spatial positioning
- Cause and effect

Preferred visual vocabulary:

```text
Objects
Vectors
Arrows
Force indicators
Motion trails
Graphs
Equations
Measurement lines
Coordinate systems
```

Example:

```text
             F →
       ┌──────────┐
       │  BLOCK   │
       └──────────┘
          ↓
          mg
```

Whenever possible, animate the actual relationship being taught.

---

# 9. Mathematics

Mathematics should prioritize:

- Structure
- Transformation
- Relationships
- Spatial reasoning
- Progressive construction

Useful visual techniques:

- Equation construction
- Geometric transformation
- Graph animation
- Coordinate movement
- Shape decomposition
- Number-line movement
- Highlighted variables
- Step-by-step derivation

Example:

```text
x² + 2x + 1

      ↓

(x + 1)²
```

The transformation itself should be visually demonstrated.

---

# 10. Biology

Biology should use layered visual explanations.

Preferred techniques:

- Anatomical diagrams
- Cross-sections
- Zoom transitions
- Microscopic views
- Cell structures
- Biological processes
- Organ-system relationships

Example:

```text
Human body
     ↓
Organ
     ↓
Tissue
     ↓
Cell
     ↓
Organelle
```

Lumo should be able to move between scales when useful.

---

# 11. Chemistry

Chemistry visuals should prioritize:

- Molecular structure
- Bonding
- Reactions
- Particle behavior
- Atomic relationships

Preferred visual vocabulary:

- Molecular models
- Particle diagrams
- Electron representations
- Bond formation
- Reaction transitions
- State changes

Avoid making chemistry look like decorative laboratory imagery.

The molecules and reactions are the focus.

---

# 12. History

History should prioritize:

- Chronology
- Geography
- People
- Events
- Cause and effect
- Political changes
- Cultural context

Useful visual formats:

```text
Timeline
Map
Portrait / figure
Event card
Territory transition
Historical environment
Artifact
```

Example:

```text
1200 ───── 1400 ───── 1600 ───── 1800
          │
          │
       EVENT
```

Historical visuals should feel editorial and contextual rather than cartoonish.

---

# 13. Geography

Geography should make spatial relationships obvious.

Preferred formats:

- Maps
- Terrain
- Climate zones
- Rivers
- Mountains
- Cities
- Population maps
- Environmental scenes

Maps should remain legible and avoid unnecessary decorative detail.

---

# 14. Computer Science & Programming

Programming visuals should feel technical but approachable.

Preferred formats:

```text
Code
  ↓
Execution
  ↓
Memory / State
  ↓
Output
```

Useful visualizations:

- Code execution
- Variable state changes
- Data structures
- Memory
- Network flow
- Architecture
- Algorithms
- Trees
- Graphs
- Databases
- APIs
- Request/response cycles

For programming lessons, the visual stage can combine:

- Code editor
- Diagram
- Runtime visualization
- Terminal output
- Animated data flow

Avoid turning every programming lesson into a generic code screenshot.

---

# 15. Environmental Scenes

Lumo should use environmental scenes when they make an abstract concept concrete.

Example:

Instead of explaining force entirely through abstract arrows:

```text
Force → mass → acceleration
```

the tutor could show:

```text
Person
   ↓
pushes
   ↓
shopping cart
   ↓
cart accelerates
```

The AI can then transition from the real-world example into the formal diagram.

This creates a useful teaching progression:

> Real world → visual abstraction → formal concept.

# 17. AI Tutor Visual Direction

The AI tutor is a guide, not the lesson itself.

The visual stage should remain the primary focus during teaching.

Avoid:

```text
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│                              │
│          AI AVATAR           │
│                              │
│       "Hello student..."     │
│                              │
└──────────────────────────────┘
```

Preferred:

```text
┌───────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                           │
│             LESSON VISUAL                 │
│                                           │
│             F = ma                        │
│                                           │
│       object ─────────→ force             │
│                                           │
│                              ┌─────────┐  │
│                              │  TUTOR  │  │
│                              │         │  │
│                              └─────────┘  │
└───────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The AI tutor should visually communicate:

- Presence
- Attention
- Expression
- Confidence
- Warmth
- Responsiveness

But it should not constantly compete with the educational visualization.

# 18. AI Tutor Animation

Future AI tutor implementations may support:

- Lip synchronization
- Facial expressions
- Eye movement
- Head movement
- Natural gestures
- Speaking states
- Listening states
- Thinking states
- Emotional feedback

The tutor should have distinct states:

```text
IDLE
LISTENING
THINKING
SPEAKING
INTERRUPTED
CELEBRATING
ENCOURAGING
ASSESSING
```

These states should feel subtle and natural.

Avoid exaggerated cartoon animation unless the selected age/personality mode specifically requires it.

# 19. AI Tutor Personality by Age

The visual system should support adaptive tutor presentation.

## Younger students

Can use:

- More expressive facial animation
- Slightly more playful movement
- Friendlier visual language
- Larger visual cues
- More expressive reactions

## Older students

Should use:

- More restrained animation
- More sophisticated presentation
- Technical diagrams
- More compact UI
- More professional tutor presence

The underlying Lumo visual identity must remain consistent.

---

# 20. 2D vs 3D Rules

Use 2D when:

- The concept is primarily symbolic
- A diagram communicates it clearly
- Precision matters
- The subject is textual or conceptual
- 3D would add unnecessary complexity

Use 3D when:

- Spatial relationships matter
- Rotation matters
- Depth matters
- Scale matters
- Physical interaction matters
- A structure benefits from exploration
- The concept is naturally three-dimensional

---

# 21. 3D Technology Philosophy

Lumo should NOT use 3D everywhere.

3D should be treated as a specialized educational capability.

Potential technologies may include:

- React Three Fiber
- Three.js
- WebGL
- GPU-accelerated rendering
- Custom interactive simulations

These technologies should be introduced only where they meaningfully improve the educational experience.

Examples:

```text
Cell
→ 3D structure

Solar system
→ 3D spatial relationships

Molecule
→ 3D bonds

Geometry
→ 3D transformations

Physics
→ interactive physical simulation
```

The standard lesson UI should remain lightweight.

---

# 22. Scientific Diagram Standards

Scientific diagrams must prioritize accuracy and clarity.

Requirements:

- Correct proportions where relevant
- Correct relationships
- Clear labels
- Consistent symbols
- Meaningful arrows
- Logical hierarchy
- No decorative objects that could be mistaken for meaningful information

Labels should appear close to the object they describe.

Use signaling to draw attention to the active concept.

Example:

```text
        MITOCHONDRION
              ↓
        ┌───────────┐
        │           │
        │  CELL     │
        │           │
        └───────────┘
```

---

# 23. Visual Signaling

Lumo should use visual signaling to guide attention.

Possible techniques:

- Highlight
- Glow
- Focus
- Zoom
- Motion
- Color shift
- Outline
- Arrow
- Spotlight
- Progressive reveal

Use one or two signaling techniques at a time.

Avoid making everything glow.

If everything is highlighted, nothing is highlighted.

---

# 24. Progressive Visual Disclosure

Complex visuals should be constructed progressively.

Example:

### Step 1

Show object.

### Step 2

Introduce force.

### Step 3

Introduce direction.

### Step 4

Introduce acceleration.

### Step 5

Introduce equation.

### Step 6

Connect equation to the observed behavior.

This mirrors the teaching process.

---

# 25. Animation and Visualization Synchronization

Visuals should be synchronized with tutor narration whenever technically possible.

Example:

Tutor says:

> "When resistance increases..."

At that moment:

```text
Resistance
     ↑
```

appears.

Then:

```text
Current
     ↓
```

appears.

Then:

```text
V = IR
```

is revealed.

This makes animation part of the explanation rather than decoration.

---

# 26. Cinematic Visuals

Cinematic visuals should be used for:

- Lesson introductions
- Real-world examples
- Major concept transitions
- Subject introductions
- Story-driven explanations
- Historical environments
- Scientific environments

Cinematic does NOT mean:

- Excessive particles
- Lens flares
- Constant camera movement
- Heavy gradients
- Flashy transitions
- Decorative effects

The goal is:

> **Immersion without distraction.**

---

# 27. Visual Transitions

Preferred transitions:

- Fade
- Crossfade
- Morph
- Camera movement
- Zoom
- Object transformation
- Spatial transition
- Progressive construction

Avoid:

- Random wipes
- Excessive bouncing
- Flash transitions
- Overly dramatic effects
- Constant zooming

Transitions should communicate relationships whenever possible.

# 30. Asset Metadata

Future assets should ideally contain metadata such as:

```text
assetId
category
subject
tags
style
ageSuitability
dimensions
3D / 2D
animationCapabilities
interactionCapabilities
license
source
```

Example:

```json
{
  "assetId": "physics-shopping-cart",
  "category": "vehicle",
  "subject": ["physics"],
  "tags": ["force", "motion", "friction", "mass"],
  "style": "editorial-2.5d",
  "ageSuitability": "all"
}
```

This allows the AI teaching system to retrieve appropriate visual assets intelligently.

---

# 31. Asset Consistency

Assets used together should share:

- Similar proportions
- Similar lighting
- Similar rendering style
- Similar line weight
- Similar perspective
- Similar level of detail

Do not combine:

```text
photorealistic car
+
flat cartoon person
+
low-poly tree
+
hand-drawn arrow
```

unless the lesson intentionally uses a mixed-media visual style.

---

# 32. Generated vs Library Assets

Lumo should use a hybrid strategy.

## Reusable library assets

Best for:

- Common objects
- Frequently used diagrams
- Common scientific structures
- Standard environments
- Repeated visual motifs

## Generated assets

Best for:

- Unique scenarios
- Unusual environments
- Historical scenes
- Custom examples
- Highly specific lesson requirements

## Procedural / interactive assets

Best for:

- Mathematics
- Physics
- Simulations
- Geometry
- Graphs
- Data
- Algorithms

The system should choose the appropriate method instead of forcing one technology onto every visual.

---

# 33. Visual Accuracy

Educational visuals must never sacrifice correctness for aesthetics.

When accuracy matters:

> Correctness > beauty.

This is especially important for:

- Biology
- Chemistry
- Physics
- Mathematics
- Engineering
- Programming
- Geography

The AI should not invent scientifically misleading visual representations simply because they look attractive.

---

# 34. Visual Simplicity

The amount of visual detail should correspond to the student's current learning objective.

If the lesson is:

> "What is a force?"

Do not show an elaborate city scene containing twenty objects.

Show:

```text
Object
+
Applied force
+
Motion
```

Once the student understands the basic concept, environmental complexity can increase.

---

# 35. Backgrounds

Backgrounds should support the subject.

Preferred:

- Soft neutral environments
- Subtle gradients
- Light paper-like textures
- Simple scientific environments
- Minimal classroom environments
- Carefully designed real-world scenes

Avoid:

- Busy backgrounds
- Strong patterns
- Decorative particles
- Excessive visual noise
- Background elements competing with diagrams

---

# 36. Light Theme Visuals

Lumo's light theme should feel:

> Fruity, fresh, optimistic, premium and spacious.

Visuals may use:

- Soft fruit-inspired accents
- Fresh secondary colors
- Gentle tinted surfaces
- Warm neutrals
- Bright educational diagrams

However, color must remain functional.

Do not turn the learning stage into a rainbow.

---

# 37. Dark Theme Visuals

The dark theme should feel:

> Cinematic, focused, sophisticated and immersive.

The logo may switch to white for contrast.

Educational visuals should remain highly legible.

Avoid excessive neon.

Avoid making every element glow.

Dark mode is not:

> "Cyberpunk AI."

It is:

> **A focused night-learning environment.**

---

# 38. Color Usage in Illustrations

Color should communicate meaning whenever possible.

For example:

```text
Primary concept
→ primary Lumo accent

Secondary concept
→ secondary accent

Correct / positive
→ success treatment

Attention
→ amber treatment

Error / misconception
→ soft coral treatment
```

Never rely on color alone to communicate meaning.

Combine color with:

- Labels
- Icons
- Position
- Shape
- Motion
- Text

---

# 39. Avoid AI-Slop Visuals

Lumo must actively avoid visual patterns associated with generic AI-generated interfaces.

Avoid:

- Floating purple blobs
- Random glowing orbs
- Generic humanoid robots
- Excessive holograms
- Random neon gradients
- Generic futuristic classrooms
- Unexplained 3D spheres
- Abstract AI brains
- Decorative neural networks
- Excessive glassmorphism

If an effect does not communicate something, remove it.

---

# 40. Visual Storytelling

For difficult concepts, use a visual narrative.

A useful pattern is:

```text
REAL WORLD
    ↓
OBSERVATION
    ↓
SIMPLIFIED MODEL
    ↓
FORMAL DIAGRAM
    ↓
EQUATION / RULE
    ↓
APPLICATION
```

Example:

```text
Shopping cart
      ↓
Push
      ↓
Acceleration
      ↓
Force diagram
      ↓
F = ma
      ↓
What happens if mass doubles?
```

This is one of Lumo's strongest opportunities to differentiate itself.

---

# 41. Visual Continuity

Visuals should not unnecessarily reset between every tutor turn.

If the tutor continues discussing the same concept:

- Modify the existing visual
- Highlight the relevant element
- Add information
- Transform the diagram
- Zoom into the relevant section

Do not repeatedly replace the entire scene.

This creates the feeling of a continuous classroom rather than disconnected AI generations.

---

# 42. Visual State Awareness

The visual system should understand the current teaching state.

For example:

```text
LISTENING
→ visual remains stable

THINKING
→ subtle tutor activity

SPEAKING
→ visual progression begins

INTERRUPTED
→ active animation stops immediately

QUESTION
→ visual focuses on relevant concept

ASSESSMENT
→ visual simplifies and reduces distraction

REPLAY
→ previous visual state restored

RE-EXPLAIN
→ same concept, new visual approach
```

This connects the illustration system directly to Lumo's orchestration architecture.

---

# 43. Assessment Visuals

Assessment visuals should be simpler than teaching visuals.

The student's cognitive focus should be:

> Understand the question → Think → Answer.

Avoid excessive animation during assessment.

Use visuals only when they are part of the question.

Example:

```text
        10 N →
   ┌───────────┐
   │   BLOCK   │
   └───────────┘

What is the acceleration?
```

The visual should support the question.

---

# 44. Interactive Visuals

When appropriate, students should be able to interact with educational visuals.

Examples:

### Physics

Drag an object.

Change force.

Change mass.

Observe acceleration.

### Mathematics

Move a point.

Change an angle.

Observe the graph.

### Chemistry

Combine molecules.

Observe reaction.

### Biology

Rotate a cell.

Zoom into organelles.

### Geography

Explore terrain.

Toggle layers.

Interactive visuals should provide meaningful feedback.

---

# 45. Responsive Visual Design

Educational visuals must work across:

- Desktop
- Laptop
- Tablet
- Mobile

Important content should remain visible at smaller sizes.

Do not create diagrams that depend entirely on large horizontal screens.

Where necessary:

- Recompose
- Crop intelligently
- Simplify
- Stack
- Zoom
- Change camera position

Do not simply shrink everything.

---

# 46. Performance

Visual quality must not destroy usability.

Prefer:

- Lazy loading
- Asset caching
- Compression
- Progressive loading
- GPU acceleration where appropriate
- Reusable assets
- Lightweight SVGs
- Optimized 3D models
- Controlled particle counts

Heavy 3D scenes should not load on pages that do not need them.

---

# 47. Fallback Strategy

Every advanced visual should have a fallback.

If:

```text
3D model unavailable
```

use:

```text
2.5D diagram
```

If:

```text
environment unavailable
```

use:

```text
simplified diagram
```

If:

```text
animation fails
```

show:

```text
static educational frame
```

If:

```text
asset generation fails
```

use:

```text
library asset
```

Learning should never depend entirely on a single visual asset.

---

# 48. Visual Quality Checklist

Before shipping a visual, ask:

### Educational

- Does it teach something?
- Is the concept obvious?
- Is the hierarchy clear?
- Is the visual accurate?

### Cognitive

- Is there unnecessary detail?
- Is the student being asked to look at too many things?
- Is the important information clearly signaled?

### Aesthetic

- Does it feel premium?
- Does it belong to Lumo?
- Does it avoid generic AI aesthetics?

### Technical

- Is it performant?
- Is it responsive?
- Does it have a fallback?

### Accessibility

- Is important information understandable without color?
- Is text readable?
- Are labels sufficiently contrasted?
- Can motion be reduced?

---

# 49. The Lumo Visual Rule

Every visual should follow this principle:

> **If removing the visual makes the concept harder to understand, keep it.**

If removing it changes nothing:

> **Remove it.**

This should be one of the strongest rules in the entire Lumo design system.

---

# 50. Final Art Direction

Lumo's educational visuals should ultimately feel like:

> **A premium interactive textbook brought to life by an intelligent tutor.**

The student should feel that concepts are not merely being shown to them.

They are being **revealed**.

A formula becomes a physical relationship.

A cell becomes a living structure.

A historical event becomes a timeline and environment.

A programming concept becomes an executing system.

A mathematical equation becomes something the student can manipulate.

A difficult topic becomes something the student can finally see.

That is the visual identity of Lumo.

---

# 51. One-Line Visual Philosophy

> **Make the invisible visible. Make the abstract tangible. Make learning feel alive.**
